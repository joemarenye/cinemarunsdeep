I Was Ready to Judge

The hardest audience for Toy Story 5 was never critics. It was everyone who grew up with the first one.

By hazardous10 · July 12, 2026 · 9 min read

I walked into Toy Story 5 already defensive. Not curious. Not hopeful. Defensive. Because this is not just another movie. This is Toy Story. This is childhood. This is Saturday mornings, worn-out DVDs, and knowing every line before it was said. This is Woody and Buzz — not characters, but constants. So when the lights dimmed, I was not ready to watch. I was ready to judge.

And then it started.

That familiar score crept in — not loudly, not trying to overwhelm — but just enough to tap something buried. And suddenly I was not in a cinema anymore. I was back in that space where toys mattered more than anything real.

I. The Weight of Coming Back

Toy Story 3 ended perfectly. That is not an opinion. It is a widely held emotional fact. Andy going to college, the incinerator scene, the toys finding their way to Bonnie — it was a conclusion so complete that many people left the cinema genuinely unsure whether they wanted another one. The story felt finished. The characters felt resolved. And yet here we are.

The pressure on Toy Story 5 was unlike anything most sequels face. It was not just competing with its predecessors. It was competing with memory. With the version of these characters that has lived in people's heads for decades, unchanged, perfect, preserved exactly as they were when they first mattered. No film can win that competition directly. You cannot out-nostalgia nostalgia. You can only try to earn your place alongside it.

Making a sequel to a perfect ending is not a creative decision. It is a gamble. Toy Story 5 knew that and made it anyway.

What makes that gamble interesting is the question the film chose to ask instead of trying to recapture what Toy Story 3 already did. Rather than chasing the same emotional territory, Toy Story 5 arrives with something quieter and in some ways harder to sit with. It does not ask whether these characters can find a new home or a new purpose. It asks something more uncomfortable. What does it mean to matter when the world has moved on without you? That is a different question entirely. And it hits differently depending on how old you are when you hear it.

II. What The Film Actually Does

The first time Woody and Buzz share the screen again, you feel it. Not in a casual “oh nice” kind of way — but in that quiet, heavy way where nostalgia does not shout. It presses down on your chest.

Woody feels older. Not tired — just aware. Like someone who understands that being needed is not guaranteed anymore. There is a specific kind of sadness in watching a character who spent four films defined by his loyalty and purpose quietly reckon with what happens when that purpose becomes uncertain. He is not broken. He is just paying attention to something he was not ready to see before. And the film gives him the space to do that without rushing toward a resolution that would feel false.

Buzz carries something different. There is a quiet loyalty in him that has not changed, but now it feels heavier. More deliberate. Like someone choosing every day to show up for something they believe in even when the belief costs them something. The friendship between Woody and Buzz has always been the emotional core of this franchise. In Toy Story 5 it does not feel like a centerpiece. It feels like a foundation. Quiet, load-bearing, and more important than anything built on top of it.

Nostalgia does not shout. It presses down on your chest. Toy Story 5 knew that. And it used it.

Visually, the film is something else entirely. Pixar did not just animate this film. They crafted it. Every surface, every shadow, every tiny detail feels like it exists in a physical space. There were moments watching it where the animation stopped feeling like animation and started feeling like memory. Not photorealism exactly. Something more specific than that. The feeling of a place you have been before rendered with more clarity than you could manage yourself.

There are new characters. Some land instantly. Some take time. But that is not why you are there. You are there for that feeling — that impossible balance between growing up and holding on. And the film delivers it without apology and without manipulation. It earns every emotional moment it asks you to sit with.

III. The Question The Film Is Really Asking

What does it mean to matter when the world has moved on without you?

On the surface that is Woody's question. His story. His reckoning. But Pixar has always been too precise to aim a question that specifically at a toy and mean it only for the toy. The real audience for that question is sitting in the cinema holding a bucket of popcorn and trying to remember the last time they felt as certain about something as they did when they first watched a cowboy doll and a space ranger become friends.

At eight years old you watched Toy Story and understood it as an adventure. At twenty five you watch the same film and recognize it as something closer to a mirror. The fear of being replaced. The need to feel useful. The quiet terror of becoming someone whose best days are behind them without ever quite noticing it happen. Woody was never just worried about a toy. He was worried about the same things every adult worries about when they are honest enough to admit it.

You are not the same person who watched the first Toy Story. And neither are they. Toy Story 5 does not ignore that. It leans into it. Hard.

That is what makes this film land differently depending on when you came to this franchise. A child watching Toy Story 5 sees a story about toys going on an adventure. An adult who grew up with Woody and Buzz sees something closer to a conversation about what it means to age gracefully, to stay relevant, to hold onto the relationships that defined you while accepting that the world you built them in no longer exists in quite the same way. Pixar knew exactly who was sitting in that cinema. They always do. And they wrote directly to that person without ever making it feel like therapy.

By the time it ended I was not thinking about plot holes or whether this sequel was necessary. I was just quiet. Processing. Feeling that strange mix of gratitude and sadness that only this franchise seems to pull off.

Was Toy Story 5 perfect? No. Did it need to exist? Maybe not. But am I glad it does? Yeah. Because somehow, against all odds, it does not just revisit the past. It reminds you why it mattered in the first place. It gives Woody and Buzz one more chapter without pretending it is the last one or the most important one. It just gives them more time. And for everyone who grew up watching them, that turns out to be enough. More than enough.

— hazardous10
